🇵🇱 Poland

Malvina Kaplan (1913–1987)

Country of Origin: Poland

Status: Holocaust Survivor

Biography

Malvina Kaplan (née Kaminer), who sometimes spelled her name Malwina or Melvina Kaplan, was born in 1913 in Łódź, Poland, into a religious family that nevertheless supported her artistic inclinations. As a child, a Polish painter visited the family home and taught her to paint. In 1937 she was sent to study at the Academy of Fine Arts in Warsaw, but was forced to abandon her studies when World War II broke out in 1939. In 1940 she married Moshe Kaplan and moved to Częstochowa, where she and her husband were confined to the ghetto.

War Years, Deportation, and Rescue

Shortly before the liquidation of the ghetto, Malvina and her husband obtained forged documents and, posing as Poles, fled to Germany, where they worked on a farm and in a factory. In 1944 her Jewish identity was discovered, and she was deported to Auschwitz, and from there to Ravensbrück and Malchow.

In the spring of 1945 she was saved by Yora Tamkin, a Jew whose parents had converted to Christianity and who later rediscovered his Jewish identity. Tamkin came to the Dübendorf camp, selected a group of twelve Jews, and brought them to Stockholm, Sweden. There he housed them in a villa, provided for their livelihood, and offered them a renewed cultural life so that they might "learn to laugh again." In Stockholm, Malvina worked in a studio for applied arts, reunited with her husband, and in 1947 the couple immigrated to Israel.

Life in Israel

In Israel, Malvina divided her time between Tel Aviv, where she lived, and Safed, where she painted extensively. Seeking to free herself from the Polish Impressionism in which she had been trained, she studied at the Avni Institute in Tel Aviv and became a member of the Israel Painters and Sculptors Association.

Artistic Style and Subject Matter

At the outset of her career, Kaplan was influenced by the Polish Impressionist painting in which she had grown up. After arriving in Israel, she gradually moved away from that tradition and connected with the modernist currents developing in the country.

In her early modernist period, her work was largely figurative, consisting mainly of impressions of the country's desert landscapes. These paintings were executed in vivid, warm reds and yellows, applied dynamically and sharply. Although figurative, a tendency toward abstraction was already evident.

In the mid-1960s she began creating collages, carefully composed in both content and design, and devoted a solo exhibition to the medium. She used papers on which she had painted, rather than newspaper clippings or writing paper. These works typically connected her Holocaust experiences on one hand with the joy of life in Israel on the other.

In the 1970s her paintings were largely abstract, with intense, luminous color arrangements. She sought to bring balance and calm to her compositions, yet often found it difficult to restrain the flow of emotion in her work. She was known for oils, mixed media, watercolors, and pastels.

Collections

Works by Malvina Kaplan are documented in the Information Center for Israeli Art at the Israel Museum, Jerusalem, and appear in public and private collections in Israel.
