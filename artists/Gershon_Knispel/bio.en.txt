🇩🇪 Germany

Gershon Knispel (1932–2018)

Country of Origin: Germany

Status: Jewish Refugee from Nazi Germany

Biography

Gershon Knispel was born on September 11, 1932, in Cologne, Germany, to Chaim and Aliza Knispel. In 1935, following the rise of the Nazis to power, he immigrated with his family to Eretz Israel. He studied art at the New Bezalel School of Art in Jerusalem in the early 1950s and went on to create lithographs and engravings for poetry books, including illustrations for Alexander Penn, Bertolt Brecht, and Avraham Zhitomirsky. He was a member of the Communist Party in both Israel and Brazil and remained active in left-wing political circles throughout his life.

War Years and Emigration

Knispel left Germany at the age of three with his family as part of the wave of Jewish emigration from Nazi Germany. He grew up in the Yishuv and came of age as an artist in the young State of Israel.

Life and Career in Israel

As a leading figure in Israeli social realism, Knispel was among the few artists who directly addressed the living conditions and difficult absorption of tens of thousands of Holocaust survivors and Mizrahi immigrants. He also dealt extensively with Holocaust themes in painting and monumental public art.

In his famous painting Labor Exchange (1956), he depicted unemployed workers crowding the locked doors of the labor bureau. Haifa mayor Abba Hushi appealed to the Foreign Ministry to prevent the work from being sent to the Youth Biennale in Moscow; after a stormy public debate, the painting mysteriously disappeared en route. A monumental version of Labor Exchange is held in the collection of the Haifa Museum of Art. Knispel characteristically worked on several versions of the same subject in parallel.

His first solo exhibition opened in 1956 at the Katz Gallery of Art, featuring his illustrations for Alexander Penn's poems from the poet's first book, Along the Way, published by the Israeli Communist Party. The exhibition was recreated in 2013 by curator Ron Bartosh and opened on May 1 at the Hanina Gallery in Tel Aviv.

The Brazilian Period and Return

In 1959 Knispel moved to Brazil after winning a competition to design the façade of a television tower in São Paulo. He remained there for several years, collaborating closely with the architect Oscar Niemeyer and creating dozens of public works — outdoor sculptures, murals, and façade reliefs on public buildings across Brazilian cities. He also turned to monumental art rooted in the Holocaust, mainly though not exclusively of the Jewish people.

When a military coup broke out in Brazil in 1964, Knispel, as a Communist Party member, became wanted by the new regime. He fled his home and reached the Israeli Embassy in Rio de Janeiro hidden in the trunk of a car, leaving many works behind on public buildings throughout Brazil.

Knispel settled in Haifa and served as artistic consultant to the municipality from 1964 to 1989, placing numerous works in the city's public spaces. Among his major Haifa works are the stone relief on Paz Bridge, the bas-relief on the Romema Sports Hall, and the Monument to the Jewish Fighter in World War II (1991). Together with the Palestinian artist Abed Abdi he created the Land Day monument in Sakhnin (1976).

In 1995, after the fall of Brazil's military dictatorship, Knispel returned to Brazil and divided his life between Haifa and São Paulo.

Later Years and Legacy

In 2012, after tiles fell from a bas-relief Knispel had created on Haifa Municipal School No. 5, the municipality removed and discarded the work. Knispel sued the city and ultimately received compensation from the Supreme Court. In 2016 a comprehensive retrospective volume on his work was published in Hebrew, Portuguese, and English. In December 2017 the book won first place in Brazil's Jabuti Prize — Knispel became the first Israeli to receive this major Latin American literary award.

In 2018 his large painting Signing the Peace Petition in Acre (1953), confiscated by the Brazilian secret police during the dictatorship and later returned to him, entered the collection of the Tel Aviv Museum of Art after extensive restoration. It is displayed in the museum's permanent exhibition of Israeli art.

Knispel remained until his last days a fiercely anti-establishment artist who nonetheless received broad institutional recognition for the importance of his work. He died in Haifa on September 7, 2018, at the age of eighty-five, survived by three children and grandchildren.

Artistic Style and Subject Matter

Knispel worked as a painter, sculptor, printmaker, and designer of monumental public art. His style is associated with social realism: figurative, politically engaged, and often large in scale. He used concrete, stone, and metal alongside painting and graphic media. His subjects included unemployment and class struggle, Holocaust memory, immigration and detention, national rebirth, and the shared struggles of Jews and Palestinians.

Collections

Works by Gershon Knispel are documented in the Information Center for Israeli Art at the Israel Museum, Jerusalem. Labor Exchange is in the Haifa Museum of Art. Signing the Peace Petition in Acre is in the Tel Aviv Museum of Art. His public monuments and reliefs remain installed across Haifa, Israel, and Brazil.
