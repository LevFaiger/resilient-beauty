🇩🇪 Germany

Isidor Ascheim (1891–1968)

Country of Origin: Germany

Status: Jewish Refugee from Nazi Germany

Biography

Isidor Ascheim was born in 1891 in Schreiberhau, Silesia, then part of the German Empire (today Poland), into a Jewish family. He studied at the State Academy of Art and Applied Arts in Breslau, where he was taught by Otto Mueller, one of the leading figures of German Expressionism.

During the 1920s and 1930s, Ascheim became a well-known painter and printmaker in Germany. His works were exhibited in museums and galleries, and he was regarded as one of the notable representatives of German Expressionism. He created landscapes, portraits, biblical compositions, and graphic works distinguished by emotional intensity and a highly personal style.

Nazi Persecution and Escape to Eretz Israel

After the Nazis came to power in 1933, Ascheim, as a Jew, was prohibited from teaching, exhibiting, and freely pursuing his artistic career. As antisemitic persecution intensified, his situation became increasingly difficult.

In 1937, Nazi authorities confiscated ten of his works from museums in Breslau as part of the campaign against so-called “Degenerate Art” (Entartete Kunst). Most of these works were later destroyed. Among them were the paintings Village Landscape and Harbor Scene, the watercolors Village and Country House, as well as several drawings and pastels.

In 1940, Ascheim succeeded in escaping Germany and reached Eretz Israel through illegal immigration. Upon arrival, he was interned by the British authorities in the Atlit detention camp, where he spent approximately seven months. After his release, he settled in Jerusalem and resumed his artistic work.

Artistic Style and Subject Matter

Isidor Ascheim belonged to the generation of German Expressionist artists. His teacher Otto Mueller had a significant influence on his artistic development. His works are characterized by expressive lines, emotional depth, and a strong interest in the inner world of the human figure.

His art includes landscapes, scenes of everyday life, biblical subjects, and portraits. Alongside painting, he was deeply involved in graphic arts, lithography, and printmaking.

After immigrating to Israel, Ascheim continued to develop his artistic language, combining the traditions of European Expressionism with the landscapes and spirit of his new homeland.

Collections and Legacy

Following the establishment of the State of Israel, Ascheim became one of the leading figures in Israeli art education. He taught at the Bezalel Academy of Arts and Design and served as its director between 1960 and 1961.
