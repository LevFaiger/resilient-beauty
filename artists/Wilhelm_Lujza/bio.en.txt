🇭🇺 Hungary

Lujza Wilhelm (1911–1978)

Country of Origin: Hungary

Status: Holocaust Survivor

Biography

Lujza Wilhelm (Hungarian: Wilhelm Lujza) was born on January 2, 1911, in Szombathely in the Austro-Hungarian Empire, into an Orthodox Jewish family. She was the daughter of Sándor Wilhelm, a textile merchant, and Bertha Binet. She began to illustrate and paint in childhood and took part in several painting competitions in her hometown. She was later taught by István Jászka, a painter in Szombathely.

War Years and Emigration

Her father died in August 1939. Her mother was deported and murdered at Auschwitz. As a young woman Wilhelm moved to Budapest — a step that ultimately saved her from deportation to Auschwitz. She studied at the private school of István Orkeny Streicher and afterward with Vilmos Perlrott-Csaba.

In January 1940 the Jewish Hungarian newspaper published a long report on the first art exhibition of Omike — the National Jewish-Hungarian Association for Education, which provided artistic activity for Jewish artists who could not exhibit or perform before a non-Jewish audience. The report included the following passage:

"... there also appeared an unknown talent: a village girl, Lujza Wilhelm, who arrived in Budapest with thirty pengő in her pocket, and one of her pictures was bought immediately at the exhibition — for much more than that."

After her first exhibition she took part in three additional traveling art exhibitions of Omike. Some of her works were published in the journal Múlt és Jövő (Past and Future).

After World War II she exhibited in the fine art exhibitions of the Hungarian Electric Company, which were reported in the leading national newspapers Népszava and Szabad Nép. She soon left Hungary and spent two years in Paris at the invitation of Marc Chagall. She immigrated to Israel in 1950 and settled there in Givatayim. Her husband was József Hoffman.

Life and Career in Israel

Wilhelm painted mainly in oil, gouache, and watercolor. She exhibited primarily in Tel Aviv, though her works were also shown in several European countries and in South America. Over the decades she held solo exhibitions at the Tel Aviv Museum, the São Paulo Biennale, galleries in Tel Aviv, The Hague, London, and other venues.

Wilhelm won the Nordau Prize in 1968. She died in Givatayim in 1978, at approximately sixty-six years of age.

Artistic Style and Subject Matter

Wilhelm worked across oil painting, gouache, and watercolor. Her early career in Hungary was tied to the Omike artists' movement and to the struggle of Jewish artists for visibility under restrictive conditions. After her immigration to Israel she continued to develop as a painter of figurative and still-life subjects, exhibiting widely in Israel and abroad.
