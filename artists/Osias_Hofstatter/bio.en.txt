🇵🇱 Osias Hofstatter | אוזיאש הופשטטר 🇮🇱
Painter in Oil and Ink, Holocaust Artist

December 28, 1905 – June 12, 1994

English Version
Biography
Osias Hofstatter (28 December 1905 – 12 June 1994) was a Polish-Israeli Jewish painter who worked in oil and ink. Although his art is close to Expressionism, it is most often classified as Holocaust painting. Most of his works are built from layers of ink that were scraped and scratched, from which distorted, tormented figures emerge in shades of black and rust brown.

Early Life and Wanderings
Hofstatter was born to Ben-Zion, a small-scale merchant, and Perl (née Bigleisen), a housewife, in Buchnia in Galicia — then part of Austria-Hungary. His parents had two additional children. In 1914, with the outbreak of the First World War, the region was occupied by Russia, and the Hofstatter family fled — first to Vienna, and then to Scheveningen, a fishing town near The Hague in the Netherlands. The family continued to wander, and between 1919 and 1920 Osias studied at the Realgymnasium in Frankfurt. From 1921 to 1938, the family settled in Vienna.

Persecution, War, and Camps
In 1938, following the Anschluss, the Hofstatter family was arrested. The children managed to escape, but the parents were deported to Łódź in Poland. After the Nazi occupation of Poland, they were sent from Poland to Dachau, then to Buchenwald, and afterwards all trace of them was lost.

In November 1938, Hofstatter married Anna Shbastova in Brussels. Lacking means, the couple went to live in the Belgian transit camp Marneffe, south of Brussels, established by the local Jewish committee to assist refugees.

After the Nazi occupation of Belgium in May 1940, the couple was separated: Hofstatter was sent to the Gurs forced-labor camp in southern France, while his wife was sent to another forced-labor camp, where she worked as a tailor sewing uniforms for German officers. His brother and sister found refuge in Switzerland. At Gurs, his sister was able to send him paints and money for paper. Later, his brother and sister arranged for his release, and he went into hiding near the Swiss border.

In August 1942 he tried to cross the border and was caught by the Swiss border guard. He passed through the Swiss labor camps of Aigle, Wald, and Birmensdorf. In 1943, on the strength of portrait drawings he sent to the Unitarian Service Committee in Geneva, it was decided to release him and allow him to study at the School of Art in Zurich.

After the War
In 1946 Hofstatter received a visa to Vienna, was reunited with his wife Anna, and studied at the Academy of Applied Arts. In 1948 the couple moved to Otwock in Poland, near Warsaw, where he worked as a graphic artist and taught art. In 1949 he mounted his first solo exhibition in Basel. After a decade of hardship and antisemitism, the couple decided to immigrate to Israel.

Israel: Nightwatchman and Painter
In 1957 the Hofstatters settled in Holon, where Osias earned a living as a night watchman. In April he held his first solo exhibition in Holon, and donated close to 70 of his paintings — created between 1940 and 1943 in the concentration camps — to the Ghetto Fighters' House museum.

In 1965 the writer Binyamin Tammuz discovered his work while researching his book "The Art of Israel," and organized an exhibition of Hofstatter's works at his own home. After the exhibition his works became sought after in galleries and by collectors. For the first time in his life he could devote himself fully to painting.

In 1968 the Israel Museum held a solo exhibition of his work. The curators, Yona Fischer and Elisheva Cohen, compared his paintings to those of Francisco de Goya and associated him with the Surrealist current. In 1971 his works were chosen to represent Israel at the 11th São Paulo Biennial in Brazil.

In 1970 he retired and moved to a small house in Neve Itamar on the outskirts of Netanya, where he devoted himself to painting. Five years later the couple met Yaakov and Irena Wodislawsky, also Holocaust survivors and art lovers, who offered Hofstatter to live in their apartment in Ramat Gan and pay in paintings instead of rent. There they lived until Anna's death. Afterwards Osias moved to a retirement home in Ramat Gan, where a special room was set aside for him so that he could continue to create.

Osias Hofstatter died in Ramat Gan and was buried in the Herzliya cemetery beside his wife. The couple had no children, and their entire estate was transferred to the Herzliya Museum of Art.

Artistic Style and Subject Matter
Hofstatter was an artist engaged with philosophy who searched for the essence of the human being. He tried to express that search on paper, and therefore human figures — alone or in compositions — appear in all of his works. Because of the darkness and the distorted content of his paintings, he was categorized as a "Holocaust artist." Over his lifetime Hofstatter created more than 5,000 paintings and drawings, most of them in black ink, including illustrations for the Yiddish anthology Mein Shul, which was destroyed immediately after printing because it contained criticism of Russia.

Upon his immigration to Israel he found it difficult to work because of financial hardship. From the 1950s onward he developed his distinctive technique of engraving and digging into the paper. Out of a black background emerge tormented, distorted figures whose limbs join in combinations that create movement. As he explained to Haim Gamzu about his work in 1974: "Life is torn and wounded, and I tear the paper."

He created most of his works after retiring in 1970. During that period he also began to gain recognition in Israel as an artist, and his works were shown in exhibitions and galleries. His paintings are also displayed at the Yad Vashem Memorial to the Holocaust and Heroism in Ariel.

Legacy
After Hofstatter's passing, the Wodislawsky family turned part of the Memorial to the Holocaust and Heroism in Ariel into a permanent exhibition of his paintings. In October 2013 the exhibition "That Is the Whole of Man" was mounted at the "Shem Olam" Institute in Kfar HaRoeh, telling the story of his life and including about 20 of his works that had not previously been shown.
