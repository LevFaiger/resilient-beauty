🇩🇪 Germany

Jacob Steinhardt (1887–1968)

Country of Origin: Germany

Status: Jewish Refugee from Nazi Germany

Biography

Jacob Steinhardt was born on September 25, 1887, in Zerkow, then part of the German Empire (now Poland). He received his artistic education in Berlin, where he developed into one of the leading representatives of German Expressionism. During his early career, he gained recognition for his powerful graphic works and woodcuts.

In 1933, following the rise of the Nazi regime and increasing persecution of Jews, Steinhardt immigrated to Eretz Israel with his family. He settled in Jerusalem, where he continued his artistic and educational work and became one of the central figures in the development of Israeli art. He later served as Director of the Bezalel Academy of Arts and Design.

Throughout his career, Steinhardt maintained connections with leading European artists, including Henri Matisse and Théophile Steinlen.

Nazi Persecution and Emigration

Following the Nazi rise to power, Jewish artists in Germany faced growing discrimination, restrictions, and threats. Recognizing the danger, Steinhardt left Germany and immigrated to Mandatory Palestine in 1933.

His timely departure enabled him to escape the tragic fate that later befell millions of European Jews during the Holocaust. Although he was not imprisoned in a ghetto or concentration camp, the destruction of European Jewish life profoundly influenced his worldview and artistic vision.

Artistic Style and Subject Matter

Steinhardt is best known for his expressive woodcuts and graphic works. His art is characterized by emotional intensity, dramatic contrasts, and deep spiritual content.

His subjects include biblical narratives, Jewish tradition, community life, Jerusalem landscapes, and reflections on the human condition. His work successfully combines the visual language of German Expressionism with the rich heritage of Jewish culture.

Collections

Works by Jacob Steinhardt are held in the collections of the Israel Museum in Jerusalem, the Tel Aviv Museum of Art, the British Museum, the Jewish Museum in New York, and numerous public and private collections around the world.
