Short Biography
Jakob Zim (originally Zymberknopf) was born in Sosnowiec, Poland, in 1920. He was influenced early by his father, who was a painter and placard designer. As a youth, he was a member of the Zionist youth movement, "HaNoar HaTzioni." He studied at the School of Applied Arts in Sosnowiec from 1936 to 1939.

His life was disrupted by WWII; he was sent to work in an ornamental tile factory and later deported to the Annenberg Labor Camp (1943). In 1945, he endured the "Death March" to the Buchenwald Concentration Camp, where he was liberated. Following the war, he and his brother made Aliyah to Israel via France.

In Jerusalem, he enrolled in the "New Bezalel" (Bezalel Academy of Arts and Design) from 1945 to 1947. Zim became prominent both as a graphic designer and as a respected educator, teaching graphic design at institutions like the French WIZO School and Talpiot College. He received the Shulman Prize for a Bezalel Graduate (1947) and the Prize for life achievement for contribution to Education and Art from the Ministry of Education and Culture (1982).

Painting Style and Subject Matter
Jakob Zim's work is notable for spanning two distinct creative fields: fine art (painting) and applied art (graphic design).

🖌️ Fine Art (Painting and Drawing)
Holocaust and Memory: His experiences during the Holocaust formed a central and deeply emotional theme. His wartime works and documentation of the camps, such as "View of Buchenwald, a few days after liberation" (1945, watercolor on paper), are considered vital artistic testimonies. He viewed art as a source of strength that helped him survive.

Landscape and Abstraction: Later in his career, he focused on local landscapes and more abstract subjects. Works such as "Desert Landscape," "Ruins of a Synagogue," and the abstract "Walls" and "Holes in the Negev" reflect a preoccupation with memory, the Israeli landscape, and abstraction rooted in natural forms.

Other Genres: He also created still life paintings and figurative works. His autobiography, Shards and Light, describes his work as expressing the events he lived through, coupled with an optimistic outlook: "I learned to live with the shadow and create with the light."

📐 Graphic Design
Style: Zim was part of the generation of "New Bezalel" designers who shaped the early visual language of the State of Israel, blending European influences (such as the then-popular Swiss Style) with local and Zionist motifs.

Key Projects:

Design of the symbol for the city of Netanya.

Design of the 5,000 Shekel banknote for the Bank of Israel (1984).

Design of various posters and stamps.
